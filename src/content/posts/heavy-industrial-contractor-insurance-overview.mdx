Heavy industrial contracting — mechanical, electrical, civil, and process work on refineries, chemical plants, power plants, and major industrial facilities — creates an insurance risk profile that generic contractor programs don't address well. The operating environments are hazardous, the facility owner insurance requirements are detailed and demanding, and the liability exposures are larger than most commercial construction work.

This guide covers the complete insurance program for heavy industrial contractors — every coverage line, why it matters, and how the pieces work together.

## What Makes Heavy Industrial Contractor Insurance Different

Several factors distinguish heavy industrial contractor risk from general commercial contracting:

**Operating facility environments.** Industrial contractors frequently work in operating facilities — refineries running crude, chemical plants processing reactive materials, power plants in service. These environments create hazards that non-industrial workplaces don't have: process chemical exposure, flammable atmosphere risk, high-temperature equipment, toxic gas releases, and the constant interaction between contractor operations and ongoing plant operations.

**Facility owner insurance requirements.** Major industrial operators — ExxonMobil, Dow, Duke Energy, Eastman Chemical — impose detailed contractor insurance requirements as conditions of facility access. These typically include: specific GL limits, CPL requirements, professional liability for design-build contractors, umbrella limits of $5M to $25M, specific AI endorsement forms, and ongoing certificate maintenance throughout the project.

**Scale of potential claims.** A production disruption at a major refinery during a contractor-involved incident can cost the facility owner millions of dollars per day. Property damage to process equipment can run into tens of millions. The liability exposure from a serious incident in an operating industrial facility is orders of magnitude larger than most commercial construction.

**Workforce hazard profile.** Industrial contractor crews face specific hazards: process chemical exposure, fall risk from elevated structures in complex operating environments, electrical hazards, confined space entry, and heavy equipment operation. Workers compensation exposure in operating industrial facilities is different — and more complex — than standard commercial construction.

## General Liability for Industrial Contractors

GL is the foundation of your industrial contractor program. For heavy industrial work, GL requires specific structuring:

**Completed operations coverage.** Industrial contractor work often has long-duration completed operations exposure — a modification to a process system completed this year could be alleged to have caused a failure three or four years from now. GL completed operations should extend for a period appropriate to the nature of your industrial work.

**Additional insured endorsements.** Major industrial operators require specific AI language. Common requirements include ISO CG 2010 and CG 2037 endorsements, primary and non-contributory language, and waiver of subrogation. Some operators have their own proprietary endorsement forms that differ from standard ISO language. We verify your policy meets the specific requirements of your major clients.

**Contractual liability.** Industrial contractors routinely sign indemnification agreements with facility owners — agreeing to defend and hold harmless the facility for certain claims. Your GL policy's contractual liability provision covers insured contracts (including indemnity agreements). Make sure your GL doesn't limit contractual liability in ways that create problems with your facility owner indemnity obligations.

**Coverage for property in your care, custody, or control.** Industrial contractors frequently work on and around facility owner property — process equipment, piping systems, structures. GL's "care, custody, or control" exclusion can limit coverage for damage to property in your possession. This is an area to review carefully for industrial contractors who work on and around high-value process equipment.

## Workers Compensation for Industrial Contractor Crews

Workers comp for heavy industrial contractors is one of the more complex and consequential lines in the program. The specific risks:

**Chemical exposure in operating facilities.** Industrial contractor crews work in environments where process chemicals are present — not just the contractor's own materials. Toxic vapor inhalation, skin exposure to process chemicals, and occupational disease from sustained exposure to industrial environments are covered by workers comp. Carriers who understand industrial facility operations underwrite and handle these claims more accurately.

**Falls from elevation in complex environments.** Elevated work in operating industrial facilities involves scaffolding, mobile work platforms, and permanent plant structures — in environments where the area below the work may contain operating process equipment, electrical hazards, and thermal sources. Fall incidents in these environments can be catastrophic.

**Confined space entry.** Process vessel, tank, and pipe cleaning and maintenance work often involves confined space entry under OSHA permit procedures. Oxygen deficiency, toxic atmosphere, and entrapment hazards in industrial confined spaces represent significant workers comp exposure.

**Electrical hazards.** Electrical contractor work in industrial facilities involves energized systems, high-voltage switchgear, and electrical hazards that are more severe than typical commercial electrical work.

**Process equipment injury.** Heavy mechanical work — lifting, rigging, and installing major process equipment — creates crushing, laceration, and ergonomic injury risk specific to industrial contractor operations.

Workers comp classification for industrial contractors depends on trade: pipe fitters, millwrights, electricians, boilermakers, and ironworkers each have specific classification codes, and those codes may carry different rates than general industrial painting or coating codes. Getting the classification right matters for premium accuracy and audit compliance.

## Professional Liability for Design-Build Industrial Contractors

Professional liability (E&O) applies when your industrial contracting work includes professional services — engineering input, system design, equipment specification, or professional recommendations. For process contractors, mechanical engineering firms, and design-build industrial contractors, professional liability is as important as GL.

Common triggers for industrial contractor professional liability claims:
- Process system design failures — a system you designed doesn't perform as specified
- Equipment specification disputes — the equipment you specified fails to meet process requirements
- Engineering recommendation disputes — your professional recommendation on a system modification contributes to a failure
- Installation specification errors — discrepancies between your engineered installation specifications and what was built

Professional liability is claims-made coverage — the policy in effect when the claim is filed responds, not the policy in effect when the work was done. If you provided engineering services on a major project five years ago and a claim is filed today, you need active E&O coverage today. ## Umbrella and Excess Liability

Umbrella coverage for heavy industrial contractors is almost always a necessity rather than an option. Facility owner requirements:

- Standard major industrial operators: $5M to $10M total liability
- Major refinery and chemical plant operators: $10M to $25M or higher on major projects
- Government and public utility projects: varies, often $5M to $10M

Umbrella provides these limits above your primary GL and commercial auto at a fraction of the cost of equivalent primary limits. The umbrella sits above your primary policies; when a claim exhausts your primary limits, the umbrella responds for the next layer.

For established industrial contractors, access to umbrella capacity up to $25M and above through excess layers is a program requirement, not a nice-to-have.

## Contractors Pollution Liability for Industrial Facilities

GL has a pollution exclusion that can deny coverage for third-party bodily injury and property damage arising from pollution conditions. For industrial contractors working in operating facilities, this exclusion is especially significant:

- The operating environment itself contains process chemicals and pollutants
- Your operations may disturb, release, or contribute to pollution conditions in the facility
- Third-party claims from facility workers, neighbors, or the public arising from pollution conditions may be excluded from GL

CPL covers these pollution-related third-party claims. For industrial contractors in petrochemical, chemical processing, and similar facility environments, CPL is a standard program requirement.
