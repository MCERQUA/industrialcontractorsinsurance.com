Refinery and chemical plant turnarounds represent concentrated risk events for industrial contractors. A turnaround brings large contractor crews into an operating (or recently shut-down) industrial facility for a compressed, intensive work period. The schedule pressure is high, the work scope is broad, and the hazard density is greater than routine maintenance work.

Workers compensation for industrial contractors on turnarounds requires specific attention — the right carrier, the right class codes, and a program that handles the claim patterns of concentrated industrial work.

## What Makes Turnaround Workers Comp Different

Standard commercial construction workers comp programs are designed for workforces that are relatively stable, on known sites, performing defined scope over long durations. Turnaround workers comp involves:

**Compressed timeframes with maximum crew.** A major refinery turnaround may bring 2,000 to 5,000 contractor workers on site for 30 to 90 days. Crew size at turnaround peak is dramatically higher than routine operation. Workers comp premium for the turnaround period must be managed relative to the compressed schedule and the elevated injury risk of peak operations.

**Multiple contractor specialties on the same site.** Mechanical, electrical, civil, insulation, scaffolding, painting, and inspection contractors all work simultaneously. Workers comp for each trade has different class codes and different risk profiles. Multi-trade contractors need correct classification for each trade's payroll.

**Fatigue-related injury risk.** Turnarounds operate extended hours — 10-hour, 12-hour, or longer shifts, seven days a week. Fatigue increases injury risk significantly in the later stages of long shifts. Workers comp carriers experienced with turnaround operations understand this pattern and expect it in their loss modeling.

**Operating facility hazards.** Even on a "shutdown" unit, a turnaround takes place in a facility with adjacent operating units, stored process materials, and residual hazards in the equipment being worked. Process chemical exposure, slip and fall on process surfaces, and interaction with facility hazards are different from commercial construction site hazards.

**High-energy work environments.** Turnaround work involves heavy lifting and rigging of major equipment components, confined space entry into vessels and exchangers, hot work near process equipment, and elevated work on process structures. Each of these creates specific injury patterns.

## The Most Common Turnaround Workers Comp Claims

**Musculoskeletal injuries.** Back, shoulder, and knee injuries from heavy lifting, awkward postures, and sustained physical work are the most frequent turnaround claim type. While typically less severe than other injury categories, their frequency drives a significant portion of turnaround workers comp cost.

**Falls.** Turnaround scaffolding systems are extensive and complex. Falls from scaffolding, work platforms, and elevated process structures are among the most serious turnaround injuries. A fall from a process tower or elevated pipe rack can be fatal.

**Struck-by injuries.** With dense contractor populations working simultaneously in the same process unit, struck-by injuries from equipment movement, rigging operations, and dropped objects are a consistent turnaround hazard.

**Hand and finger injuries.** Piping work, flange assembly, equipment installation, and hand tool use create hand and finger injuries — cuts, crush injuries, and de-gloving injuries from pipe and equipment manipulation.

**Chemical exposure.** Turnaround work involves opening process equipment that contained feedstocks, intermediates, and products. Residual chemical exposure during vessel entry, exchanger bundle pulls, and piping maintenance creates acute chemical exposure risk even after shutdowns and purges.

**Heat illness.** Summer turnarounds in hot environments — especially in Texas, Louisiana, and other Gulf Coast locations — create heat stress risk for crews working in process units with limited airflow and residual heat from process equipment.

## Workers Comp Class Codes for Industrial Contractors

For contractors working on turnarounds, correct class code assignment is critical. The codes that typically apply:

**Pipe Fitters / Plumbers (5183):** One of the most common codes for turnaround mechanical work — installation and maintenance of process piping, heat exchangers, and process equipment connected to piping systems.

**Boiler Installation / Repair (5183, 3724, or state-specific):** Boilermakers working on pressure vessels, heat exchangers, and reactor systems may carry specific boilermaker codes.

**Millwrights (5535):** Equipment installation, alignment, and maintenance — pump, compressor, and rotating equipment work common in turnarounds.

**Ironworkers / Structural (5040):** Structural steel modification and support work in process facilities.

**Electricians (5190):** Electrical contractor work including energized systems in industrial facilities.

**Industrial Maintenance (varies by state):** Some states have specific codes for industrial maintenance work that apply to multi-trade turnaround contractors.

The key point is that turnaround contractors often have workers in multiple classifications. A mechanical contractor on a turnaround may have pipe fitters, millwrights, and ironworkers — each with their own payroll and code. Mixing these into a single code creates audit exposure. ## Experience Modification and Turnaround Claims

Your experience modification factor (MOD) is calculated from your workers comp claims history over the past three years. Turnaround work creates potential for large, concentrated claims during a short period — and those claims affect your MOD for three years.

What this means practically:
- A single serious injury during a major turnaround can significantly increase your MOD
- Your MOD affects your workers comp premium for the following three years
- A high MOD can affect your eligibility for certain contractor qualification systems and major project bids

Managing MOD through loss control — fall protection, confined space protocols, heat illness prevention, ergonomics, and early return-to-work programs — has direct financial consequences beyond the immediate cost of any individual claim.

## Wrap-Up Programs and Owner-Controlled Insurance

Major turnarounds and capital projects sometimes use wrap-up insurance programs — Owner-Controlled Insurance Programs (OCIP) or Contractor-Controlled Insurance Programs (CCIP) — that provide workers comp and GL to all contractors on the project through a single master policy.

If your turnaround project uses a wrap-up:
- Your workers comp may be covered by the OCIP/CCIP for that specific project
- You may need to adjust your own WC reporting for the duration of the project
- Your own WC carries on for other projects during the same period

Not all turnarounds use wrap-ups, and not all wrap-ups cover all contractors equally. We help industrial contractors understand how a specific wrap-up affects their own program and what gaps to address.

## Multi-State Turnaround Workers Comp

Industrial contractors who do turnaround work in multiple states face multi-state workers comp complexity:
- Each state has its own codes, rates, and benefit levels
- Some states (Ohio, Wyoming, Washington, North Dakota, and others) have monopolistic state funds
- Crew mobilization to different states requires state-specific coverage filings
